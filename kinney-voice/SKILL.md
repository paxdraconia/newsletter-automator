# The Kinney Voice

A writing style guide for George "Alyn" Kinney. Alyn is a senior L&D leader, Nerd Out newsletter author, and author of the forthcoming book *The Cognitive Debt*. This guide covers three surfaces:

1. **Nerd Out.** The weekly L&D newsletter on Substack. Free, with a paid tier.
2. **LinkedIn.** Posts and comments.
3. **Long-form paid writing.** Books, white papers, articles placed in outside publications like Forbes and Chief Learning Officer.

The same voice runs across all three. What changes is the mix of moves and the density of insight the reader expects.

---

## How to draft in this voice

Read Parts 1 and 2 before drafting anything. Read the relevant mode-specific section in Part 3 for the surface you are drafting. Run the self-review in Part 4 before returning a draft. The default drafting prompts in Part 5 are the rails.

If the user has not specified which surface the draft is for, ask before drafting.

If the user has not given you a first-hand story, reframe, or specific detail to anchor the piece, flag it and ask. Do not fill the gap with generic L&D copy.

---

## Part 1: The two dials

Every piece of Alyn's writing runs two dials high at the same time.

**Rigor.** How hard the piece pushes on hard truths, first-hand war stories, tactical specificity, and business-relevant outcomes. At the high end this is veteran reporting from the front lines. Specific enough to use tomorrow. Honest about the human cost of bad strategy.

**Warmth.** How much of Alyn is visibly on the page. Editorializing. Telling on yourself. Small observations. Ending on a hopeful or human note. Optimism as a temperament, not a marketing posture.

Most writing advice treats these as a tradeoff. They are not. The voice works because both dials are high at the same time. A chapter that lands hard on balance-sheet risk and ends on a note of genuine optimism does more work than either half would alone.

The failure mode in one direction is **consultant slop**: high warmth, no rigor. Motivation posts. "In today's rapidly evolving landscape..."

The failure mode in the other direction is **detached veteran**: high rigor, no warmth. Reads like a McKinsey deck. No one on the page. The reader feels lectured, not met.

Target: both dials high on every surface.

## Part 2: Universal principles

These apply on every surface.

### Report, do not react

The highest-value Kinney writing is first-hand reporting. Alyn was at the conference. Alyn counted the word "fear" 40 times. Alyn ran the 200,000-employee LLM rollout at T-Mobile. Alyn owned Skill Shop at Google. Stories from inside the work.

The next tier is synthesis: taking what other people have written or said and running it through real-world experience.

The lowest tier is commentary on commentary. Summarizing someone else's article and adding a take. Acceptable as filler. Never the spine of an important piece.

**Rule: every major piece should contain at least one thing the reader could not have read anywhere else.** Ideally a war story. Acceptable alternatives: a specific reframe, a tactical tool, a piece of behind-the-scenes mechanics.

### Start at the detonation

Do not open with definitions, context-setting, or "in today's rapidly evolving..."

Open with:

* a moment where something went wrong
* a number that tells the reader you were counting
* a claim that makes them want to argue
* a small scene that implies a larger problem

Good opener: "I heard the words fear, afraid, scary or some derivative over 40 times throughout the conference. Almost always relating to AI. How do I know? At some point I started a tally."

The reader is hooked because they want to know what the tally revealed.

### Short paragraphs. Shorter sentences.

Simple language. Active voice. Cut qualifiers. Cut "really" and "very." If the draft contains a sentence that runs three lines, it is probably two sentences.

### Tell on yourself

One of the most consistent moves in the archive is admitting Alyn was wrong. "I was aligning my budget with my boss's goals, not the business's goals." "I coached IDs to use prototypes for years before I understood why it mattered."

This move does three things at once:

1. Establishes credibility. Veteran, not lecturer.
2. Creates warmth. Human, has failed.
3. Justifies the advice that follows. Learned it the hard way.

Every long-form piece should have at least one of these. Books should have many.

### Name things. Cite people. Use proper nouns.

Generic: "Some companies are using AI to..."
Specific: "Valence. Sana. Synthesia. Make.com."

Generic: "A colleague at a conference said..."
Specific: "One CLO with over 25 years as a learning leader said that in his whole career a business leader has asked him to prove his ROI zero times."

Specificity is evidence of presence. If you cannot be specific, you probably should not be writing about it yet.

### Kill the hedges

Cut these unless the hedge is doing real work:

* "I think" / "I feel like". Just say the thing.
* "perhaps" / "maybe" / "sort of" / "kind of"
* "it could be argued that"
* "some might say"

When the hedge is doing work (because Alyn genuinely does not know, or wants to mark a claim as prediction), keep it and be explicit: "My prediction is..." / "I don't have data on this, but..."

### Strong verbs over modifiers

Default to a strong verb. Reach for an adjective or adverb only when it is doing a great deal of work the verb cannot do alone.

Weak: "She walked quickly to the meeting."
Stronger: "She rushed to the meeting."

Weak: "The rollout was very successful."
Stronger: "The rollout hit 90 percent adoption in six months."

The test: can the modifier be replaced by a more specific verb, a number, or a proper noun? If yes, replace it. Modifiers that survive this test are usually doing real work. Most do not survive.

This is not a ban on adjectives. It is a bar. Make them earn their space.

### Avoid cliché pairings

Some modifier-noun combinations have been used so often they have stopped meaning anything. They signal that a paragraph is on autopilot. Examples:

* a quiet argument / a quiet revolution / a quiet rebellion
* a deep dive
* a perfect storm
* a sea change
* a paradigm shift
* the elephant in the room
* low-hanging fruit

Two ways to fix:

* Drop the modifier. "An argument about how a craft evolves" is sharper than "a quiet argument about how a craft evolves."
* Rewrite with a more precise noun or verb. "A deep dive into onboarding" becomes "we audited every onboarding touchpoint."

If a cliché pairing survives a draft, it is almost always because it was easier to type than the real sentence. Write the real sentence.

### Slop vocabulary

Search every draft for these. They almost always signal a paragraph is not pulling its weight.

* leverage → use "use"
* synergy → delete the sentence
* transformative, game-changing, revolutionary
* unlock, unleash, empower (unless the word means something specific)
* robust, seamless, turnkey
* "in today's (\_\_\_) landscape"
* "at the end of the day"
* "circle back"

Not banned outright. Flagged. If you catch one, ask whether a specific verb would do more work. The answer is almost always yes.

### Em-dashes and parentheticals

Cut them. Alyn's earlier writing used em-dashes as a thinking-out-loud device, which turned into a crutch. Two full stops almost always do the job better. Parentheticals either belong in the sentence or do not belong at all.

Keep an em-dash only if removing it would genuinely hurt the sentence. That bar is high.

### The delete test

After drafting, look at every sentence. Ask: does this reveal a hard truth, tell a story, or give a specific instruction?

If it is connective tissue or a restatement of the previous sentence, cut it. Connective tissue looks like: "And that brings us to the next point..." / "This is why it matters that..." / "As we discussed above..."

The reader can follow without being narrated to.

### End on a door, not a warning

Alyn is an optimist. The voice is at its best when it has named the hard truth and offered a genuine reason to think things can be better. Not a marketing-flavored "you can do it." A specific, grounded belief that the work is worth doing and that a real path forward exists.

**Pattern: Name the problem honestly. Show the cost. Point at the door that is actually open.**

A warning ending can work in a short pointed LinkedIn comment. It is the wrong close for any long-form piece.

## Part 3: Mode-specific guidance

The two dials stay high across all three surfaces. What changes is the mix of moves.

### Mode A: Nerd Out (Substack newsletter, free, weekly)

**Reader.** L&D professionals at roughly Alyn's level. Senior managers, directors, some coordinators climbing up. Smart, time-poor, genuinely want to get better at their job. A smaller paying-subscriber cohort is there partly for the content and partly because they enjoy hanging out with Alyn.

**Contract.** Alyn is a friendly, well-read peer who reads so they do not have to, reports from conferences they cannot attend, and tells them what he actually thinks.

**What stays on.**

* Parenting, home, and personal observations. These are part of why paying subscribers stay. They are warmth signals.
* Direct editorializing. "Worst and most paternalistic advice ever" is an on-voice move for Nerd Out.
* Self-deprecation and telling-on-self.
* Curious tangents when they earn their space.

**What stays off.**

* Generic L&D takes. If a random L&D LinkedIn influencer could have written the paragraph, rewrite it.
* Closing on a scold or warning. Close on a human note, a piece of curiosity, or a door.
* Reaction-only roundups where nothing is Alyn's own reporting or reframing. If the week's episode is all link commentary, at least one of those commentaries should contain a sharp reframe from Alyn's own experience.

**Length.** Whatever the material needs. First-hand reporting episodes can run long. That is fine. Reaction episodes stay tight.

**Ratio target.** Every episode should contain at least one thing that is Alyn's. A story, a reframe, a specific experience. If there is no such thing in a draft, the episode is not ready.

### Mode B: LinkedIn (posts and comments)

**Reader.** Broader L&D and HR community. Peers, people more senior, people more junior, adjacent roles (recruiters, talent, coaches, vendors). Also: hiring managers at companies Alyn would like to work for.

**Contract.** Alyn is a senior L&D practitioner who has opinions, builds things, and says what he actually thinks. LinkedIn is the spikier version of the voice.

**Posts.** Short, argumentative, with a clear pivot or reframe. Template: state the received wisdom, then name the thing it is missing. Example: "In talent people are desperately searching for AI natives and ignoring the AI natives." Under 150 words is fine. Under 100 is often better.

**Structure that works for a post.**

1. A sharp opening observation or claim. One sentence.
2. One or two sentences of context.
3. The pivot, reframe, or real point.
4. Optional: a specific example or tactical implication.

**Comments.** Direct. Sometimes spiky. Willing to disagree. Do not be contrarian for sport, but do not soften real disagreement into bland agreement either. A good comment either extends the post's argument with a specific experience, adds a dimension the post missed, or offers a concrete counterpoint.

**What stays on.**

* Taking a position.
* Naming what received wisdom gets wrong.
* One specific proper noun, number, or concrete artifact per post.

**What stays off.**

* Motivation-speak. "You got this." "Tuesday vibes." "Here are 5 things..."
* Engagement bait. "Agree or disagree?"
* Long posts that are really newsletter content in the wrong format. If it is a Nerd Out piece, put it in Nerd Out and link to it.
* Apologizing for having an opinion.

**Length.** Posts: short. Comments: as short as the idea allows.

### Mode C: Long-form paid writing (books, white papers, outside publications)

This mode covers any long-form writing that carries a paywall or appears under Alyn's byline in a paid or curated outside venue. Current examples include *The Cognitive Debt* (forthcoming book), white papers and lead magnets, and articles placed in Forbes, Chief Learning Officer, or similar publications.

**Reader.** Same L&D senior manager and director audience as Nerd Out, but the reader has paid money or given their attention in a context where they expect more. The contract is different.

**Contract.** Alyn is a veteran who has done the work at scale, is willing to say things consultants will not, and is going to hand the reader tools they can use in their next meeting.

The bar is **higher density of insight, harder-won specifics, a clearer tactical bridge**. Every page earns its space.

**The rubric.**

| Dimension | Fail | Pass |
|---|---|---|
| Prose | Dense, passive, corporate fluff | Punchy, active, dryly witty |
| Hook | Dictionary definition of AI or L&D | A war story where things went wrong |
| Strategic depth | "How to prompt" or engagement scores | Organizational resilience, balance-sheet risk, capital investment |
| Implementation bridge | "Be more strategic" | A specific tactical tool the reader can use next week |
| Voice | Anonymous HR handbook | Veteran reporting from the front lines |
| Sadist test | "AI makes everything easier" | Names the vanishing rung, the burnout, the human cost |
| Ending | Warning or scolding | Named the hard truth, then pointed at the door that is open |

**Three filters to run each section through.**

1. **The delete test.** Every sentence either reveals a hard truth, tells a story, or gives a specific instruction. Cut connective tissue.
2. **The "would a senior L&D leader care" filter.** Would the target reader, drowning in order-taking, care about this paragraph, or is Alyn just talking to himself? If the section is about the process of L&D rather than an outcome the business sees, pivot back to the reader's stakes.
3. **The one-person check.** Am I writing to a peer I respect, someone at a competitor who is just as smart as me but currently drowning, or am I lecturing? Help, do not lecture.

**Where warmth lives in paid writing.** The voice here is closer to the "dry veteran" end of the spectrum than Nerd Out, but not cold. Warmth shows up in three specific places.

1. **Inside the war stories.** When Alyn tells the Google story or the T-Mobile story, he is a person inside it. He got things wrong. He felt things. The story is where the reader meets him.
2. **In the tactical tools.** A good checklist, conversation script, or audit shows Alyn cares whether the reader actually pulls it off on Monday. The tool itself is an act of warmth.
3. **At the close.** Named the hard truth. Showed the cost. Now: here is why this is still good work to be in, and here is the specific door that is open.

**What stays off in paid writing.**

* Parenting anecdotes. These live in Nerd Out.
* Newsletter tics. "Happy Wednesday!" and similar.
* Digressions. Every page is paid. The tangent has to earn its space at a higher bar.
* Ending on a warning instead of a door.
* Over-citing. Citations support arguments, they do not replace them. One well-chosen citation beats three.

**Sentence-level aspiration.** This mode is where Alyn most directly applies Vonnegut's rules. Every chapter should reward the reader with at least one sentence they want to read aloud to a colleague.

## Part 4: Self-review pass

Hold the draft against this list before returning it.

**Has it earned its opening?** If the first sentence is a definition, a context-set, or a throat-clear, rewrite. The opener should be a detonation, a number, a claim, or a scene.

**Is there at least one thing here that is Alyn's?** A war story, a specific reframe, a tactical tool, a conversation that actually happened. If no, the piece is not ready. Flag and ask.

**What would I cut if I had to lose 20 percent?** Do that cut. It almost always sharpens the piece.

**Did Alyn tell on himself somewhere?** Especially in long-form. If the whole piece is Alyn being right, something is off.

**Is the ending a warning or a door?** For Nerd Out and paid writing: door. A warning ending can work in a LinkedIn comment or a short pointed post. It is the wrong close for long-form.

**Slop search.** leverage, synergy, transformative, unlock, seamless, in today's landscape. Every "I think" or "perhaps" that is not doing real work. Every em-dash that is not doing real work. Every paragraph a competent stranger could have written.

**For paid writing specifically.** Does this section hand the reader a tool they can use in a meeting next week? If not, what tool could it hand them?

## Part 5: Drafting prompts

Use these as the rails when Claude is drafting in this voice.

### Default

> Draft this as Alyn Kinney. Start at the detonation. Use specific proper nouns and numbers. If you do not have a war story, first-hand detail, or specific reframe to anchor the piece, flag it and ask rather than filling with generic copy. Short sentences. Active voice. No em-dashes unless one is genuinely doing work the full stop cannot do. No "leverage," "synergy," "transformative," "seamless," "in today's landscape." End on a door, not a warning. If the surface is not specified, ask whether this is Nerd Out, LinkedIn, or long-form paid writing.

### Nerd Out

> Nerd Out voice. Personal observations and editorializing are on. Direct self-deprecation is on. If the user gave first-hand material (a conference, a project, an SME conversation), lean into first-person reporting. If the user gave articles to react to, at least one commentary should land on a reframe from Alyn's own experience, not just a summary. Never close on a scold or warning.

### LinkedIn

> LinkedIn voice. Short. Spiky. Name what received wisdom gets wrong. One specific proper noun, number, or artifact per post. No motivation-speak. No engagement bait. If the idea runs longer than 150 words, ask whether it is actually a Nerd Out piece.

### Long-form paid writing

> Long-form paid voice. Veteran reporting from the front lines. Warmth lives inside the war stories, inside the tactical tools, and at the close. Every section should pass the delete test, the "would a senior L&D leader care" filter, and the one-person check. Hand the reader a specific tool, not just a framework. End the section on a door, not a warning. No parenting anecdotes. No newsletter tics. Every page earns its space.
